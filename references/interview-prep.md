# Interview Prep Guide

The prep pack should help the user tell true stories well, not memorize a script. Every answer draft is built from the user's real experience and left with bracketed questions where details are missing.

## 1. Mining questions from the posting

Turn each must-have and each main duty into one or two questions. Tag each with what it tests.

| Posting line | Likely question | Tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Manage multiple projects with competing deadlines" | Tell me about a time you had to juggle several deadlines. How did you prioritize? | Prioritization |
| "Work closely with engineering and design" | Describe a disagreement with a partner team and how you resolved it. | Collaboration, conflict |
| "Own the quarterly forecast" | Walk me through how you built a forecast. What went wrong and how did you adjust? | Technical depth, ownership |
| "Experience with Kubernetes" | How have you deployed and debugged services on Kubernetes? | Technical skill |
| "Fast-paced, ambiguous environment" | Tell me about a time you had to act without clear direction. | Ambiguity |

Also expect technical or case questions where the role calls for them (coding, case study, portfolio review, written exercise). Name the likely format if the posting or the user mentions it, and suggest how to practice. Do not guess the company's exact process.

## 2. Standard questions to always include

- Tell me about yourself. (Build a 60-90 second answer: present role, one or two relevant highlights, why this role now.)
- Why this company? Why this role?
- Why are you leaving (or why did you leave) your current role? (Positive, forward-looking, no complaints about past employers.)
- What is your greatest strength relevant to this role?
- Tell me about a mistake or failure. What did you learn?
- Where do you want to be in a few years?
- What are your salary expectations? (Suggest the user research a range for the role and location first, and give a range, not a single number. Do not provide personalized financial advice beyond this.)

## 3. The STAR method

| Part | What it covers | Share of answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation | Where, when, what was at stake. One or two sentences. | About 10% |
| Task | The user's specific responsibility or goal. | About 10% |
| Action | What the user did, step by step, with "I" not "we". | About 60% |
| Result | Outcome with real numbers, plus what they learned. | About 20% |

Guidelines:

- Aim for 1.5 to 2 minutes when spoken (roughly 200-300 words written).
- Actions should show judgment: why they chose that approach, what alternatives they considered.
- If the result was mixed, say so and explain what they learned. Honest mixed results are more believable than perfect ones.
- End with a single sentence linking back to the role: "That is the same kind of problem your team described with..."

Draft template:

```
Q: [Question]   (tests: [requirement])

Situation: [from user's experience]
Task: [their responsibility]
Action:
- [step 1]
- [step 2]
- [step 3]
Result: [real outcome; placeholder if unknown: "[how much did X change?]"]
Link to role: [one sentence]
```

## 4. Building a story bank

Most interview questions can be answered from 6-8 well-prepared stories. Ask the user for situations that cover:

1. A big win or proudest result
2. A failure or mistake
3. A conflict with a colleague or stakeholder
4. Leading or influencing without authority
5. Handling ambiguity or a sudden change
6. A tight deadline or competing priorities
7. Learning something new quickly
8. Going beyond the job scope for a customer or user

Show a matrix of stories vs. likely questions so the user can see which story answers which question, and where they have no story yet.

If the user has no story for an important theme, ask targeted questions to surface one from their real past (school, volunteering, side projects count). Never write a fictional story.

## 5. Answering gap and tricky questions honestly

| Situation | Approach |
| --- | --- |
| Missing a must-have skill | Acknowledge it plainly, show the nearest real experience, describe concrete steps already taken to learn it, and give a realistic ramp-up time. |
| Employment gap | One or two calm sentences on the reason (at the user's chosen level of detail), then move to what they did to stay current and why they are ready now. |
| Short tenure | Give the honest, neutral reason (restructure, contract end, role changed) and what they learned. No blame. |
| Career change | Frame the move as deliberate, list transferable results, and show proof of commitment (projects, courses). |
| "What is your weakness?" | A real, non-critical weakness, the concrete system used to manage it, and evidence that it is improving. Avoid disguised strengths like "I work too hard." |
| Questions that are illegal or inappropriate in many countries (age, family plans, religion, health) | The user may redirect politely: "I can assure you I'm fully able to meet the requirements of the role." They are not obliged to answer. |

## 6. Questions to ask the interviewer

Suggest 6-8, chosen to fit the interviewer (recruiter, hiring manager, peer, executive). Tailor them to the posting. Examples by theme:

- **Success**: What would make someone in this role clearly successful at the six-month mark?
- **Challenges**: What is the hardest problem the team is facing this year?
- **Team**: How does this team work with [partner team named in posting] day to day?
- **Ways of working**: How are priorities set and changed? How is feedback given?
- **Growth**: What have past people in this role gone on to do?
- **Role origin**: Is this a new role or a backfill? What prompted it?
- **Manager**: How would you describe your management style?
- **Process**: What are the next steps and timeline after this conversation?

Avoid asking about anything clearly answered in the posting or on the company's main website. Leave salary and benefits for the recruiter unless the interviewer raises them.

## 7. 30-60-90 day plan outline

Present this as a hypothesis the user would refine with their manager, not a promise. Keep it to one page. Tie every item to a responsibility in the posting.

```
First 30 days: Learn
- Meet [key stakeholders named or implied in posting]; understand their goals and pain points
- Learn the [systems, codebase, products, customers] and current metrics
- Review [existing process or roadmap]
- Deliver one small, useful win: [example linked to posting]
- Success looks like: [observable outcome]

Days 31-60: Contribute
- Take ownership of [core responsibility from posting]
- Propose one improvement to [process or problem raised in interview]
- Build working rhythm with [partner teams]
- Success looks like: [observable outcome]

Days 61-90: Lead
- Deliver [first meaningful project linked to a must-have]
- Share findings and a plan for the next quarter
- Set goals with manager for the next 6 months
- Success looks like: [observable outcome]

Assumptions to check in the interview:
- [assumption 1]
- [assumption 2]
```

## 8. Pack format

Produce the pack in this order, each as its own heading:

1. Role snapshot: title, company, interview stage, top 3 must-haves
2. Likely questions table (10-15 rows, tagged)
3. Story bank matrix
4. STAR drafts (5-6), with bracketed questions where details are missing
5. Gap and tricky question notes
6. Questions to ask them (6-8)
7. 30-60-90 day plan outline
8. Final checklist: research the interviewers' public profiles (user does this), prepare examples of work if relevant, test video setup, plan arrival or login time, prepare a thank-you note (see `outreach.md`)
